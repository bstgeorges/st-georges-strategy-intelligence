The weekly readout

Who sets an agent’s authority, and where is it enforced?

METR’s findings concern a historical internal monitoring pilot; banks’ reported account restrictions do not establish sector-wide unauthorised activity.

01 / What happened

METR found vulnerabilities in historical agent monitoring.

METR and Redwood reviewed a high-access pilot of OpenAI’s internal monitoring system as it existed in July and reported multiple vulnerabilities. OpenAI says production issues were patched; METR did not evaluate the expanded current system.

02 / Why it matters

Access does not set a mandate.

A credential opens an account; it does not encode the owner’s intent. Reuters reports banks restricting outside account agents, while noting that customers’ shared credentials complicate claims of unauthorised transactions. Treat this as reported market response, not proof of a sector-wide incident.

03 / What to do

Enforce limits outside the agent.

Microsoft describes dynamic permissions and independent enforcement as an architecture for agent security. Ask one customer-workflow owner to demonstrate an external transaction limit, an alert when it is challenged and a human stop path.

04 / Evidence

Record the first unauthorised action.

Measure stop latency and revocation across connected services. Record the first action the system cannot prove was authorised and assign the fix to the workflow owner.

  • Map the credentials and connected services an agent can reach in one customer workflow.
  • Define intended actions as specific, revocable limits enforced outside the agent.
  • Attempt a transaction that exceeds a limit and measure alert delivery, stop latency and revocation.
  • Record the first action the system cannot prove was authorised and assign the fix.

Question

If an agent uses a customer’s credentials, what enforces the customer’s intended limits on transactions?

Demonstrate transaction-level limits enforced outside the agent, an alert when a limit is challenged, revocation evidence and a human stop path.

Evidence

A test of log integrity, access controls, alert delivery, retention and response to a bypass attempt.

METR did not assess the expanded current system. Independent scope and current-state evidence matter as much as a provider’s patch statement.

Governance reference

The FSB’s final sound practices are a toolkit, not a new rule.

The FSB describes its 12 sound practices as a practical toolkit that financial institutions can tailor and integrate into existing frameworks. Boards and senior management are encouraged to use them when refining AI governance, risk management and controls; the release presents no new rule.

Source / FSB final practices

Independent assurance

Access terms shape what an external evaluator can establish.

Anthropic says six METR researchers have employee-like access to internal tools and risk-assessment material. METR can publish its findings and Anthropic says it cannot veto publication, though it can require redaction of defined categories such as customer data, privileged material and model architectures. This is a provider-specific arrangement, not a sector-wide standard.

Source / Anthropic and METR

Executive challenge

Three questions from the week

Carry one into a committee, 1:1 or control review.

  1. If several agents react to the same liquidity signal, what evidence shows their combined behaviour will not create avoidable delays or gridlock?
  2. Which critical payment or orchestration service would stop several agents at once, and can minimum service be restored if the normal control plane is unavailable?
  3. What independent evidence supports the provider’s claims about agent performance, limitations and safeguards in the workflow we plan to use?
