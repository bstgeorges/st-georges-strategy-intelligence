When the map is redrawn: AI chip diversion and the bank's blind spot

A company's revenue map can show who was billed without showing where the chips went. Banks that finance and pay for the trade face the same blind spot.

By Ben St Georges · 12 November 2026 · 8 min read

When Nvidia changed how it reports revenue by country in late 2025, Singapore disappeared from the table. It had been about a fifth of the company's sales. Companies may choose how they attribute revenue to countries, and Nvidia says the new basis better represents its revenue. But the episode exposes a wider problem: a disclosure can tell you who was invoiced, or where a customer is headquartered, without telling you where the hardware ends up or who uses it.

Over the past year, US prosecutors, the Singapore authorities and several news organisations have described how advanced chips and servers can move through Southeast Asian intermediaries towards China. Some of this has led to criminal charges. Much of it remains allegation. None of it is confined to one company.

For banks, the question is practical. If public disclosures do not reveal where the hardware ultimately goes, how much visibility can banks obtain when they finance, pay for or lend to the intermediaries?

Compliance implication: The expectations already exist. US authorities say banks can be liable for financing an export violation they know about, and EU guidelines require banks to assess their exposure to sanctions circumvention. What is harder is evidencing that controls can see through intermediaries whose customers, funding and end users are opaque.

01 / Geographic disclosure

What geographic disclosures show and hide

Until mid-2025, Nvidia attributed revenue to countries by the customer's billing location. On that basis, Singapore accounted for $23.7bn of $130.5bn in fiscal 2025 revenue, about 18%, and 22% in the quarter to July 2025, according to The Wall Street Journal, via Techmeme. Nvidia's fiscal 2025 annual report noted that customers used Singapore to centralise invoicing, and that physical shipments to Singapore were under 2% of revenue.

In its quarterly report for the three months to October 2025, Nvidia switched to the location of customers' headquarters. It said the change provides a better representation of its revenue geography, noted that the end customer and shipping location may differ from the headquarters location, and recast prior periods. The fiscal 2026 annual report shows fiscal 2025 on the new basis: about $16.2bn more is attributed to the United States, $7.9bn more to China and $3.0bn more to Taiwan, and Singapore is no longer reported separately. Those increases total about $27.2bn, offset by the removal of Singapore's $23.7bn and a $3.5bn fall in revenue attributed to other countries. These are changes in how revenue is allocated, not in actual sales, chip destinations or Chinese demand. Reported China revenue went up, not down. What disappeared was a separate view of the Singapore invoicing channel.

Each basis answers a different question, and none answers the one a compliance function most needs: where is the hardware, and who is using it?

BasisWhat it showsWhat it misses
Billing locationWhere invoicing is centralisedWhere goods are shipped, and who uses them
Ship-to locationThe first physical destinationOnward transshipment, and remote use of capacity

The headquarters basis does align with one regulatory consideration. In May 2026, the Bureau of Industry and Security clarified licensing requirements for covered advanced computing items supplied to entities headquartered in Country Group D:5, which includes China, or Macau, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered there, even when the recipient operates elsewhere. But the headquarters of Nvidia's direct customer is not necessarily the headquarters of the ultimate recipient. Nor does this rule alone settle whether a locally owned Southeast Asian data centre may provide remote computing services to Chinese customers.

Nvidia has not said where sales to intermediaries such as Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud provider discussed below, were classified under either method. Nor is a company's public revenue geography evidence of what it knows internally about where its products go.

02 / Intermediary routes

The intermediary-hub pattern

The cases differ in status, but they share a route: hardware is bought, billed or housed in a Southeast Asian hub, and the question is where it goes next.

  • Supermicro. In March 2026, US prosecutors charged co-founder Wally Liaw and two others over about $2.5bn of servers with Nvidia GPUs allegedly routed to China through a Southeast Asian front company. Supermicro was not charged and says it was a victim. One defendant pleaded guilty in October 2026; Liaw has pleaded not guilty and his trial is set for March 2027 (Fortune).
  • Singapore server fraud. Four individuals and four companies face charges in Singapore, including fraud by falsely representing who would use servers bought from Dell, Supermicro and Asus. The individuals face further charges, including money laundering for some of them, and more than S$56m of assets, mostly a property valued at about S$55m, has been seized or is subject to a prohibition of disposal order (Singapore Police Force, 1 July 2026). The charges concern false statements about end users; they do not establish where the servers ultimately went.
  • Megaspeed. This Singapore-based neocloud is under investigation in Singapore for “suspected breaches of our domestic laws” (Reuters via AsiaOne). The New York Times reported it bought more than $4.8bn of Nvidia chips over two years; The Information alleges at least $7.5bn moved through it and a Malaysian affiliate. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. On 12 November it said it had cooperated with Bureau of Industry and Security inspections and hired a US law firm to review its GPU inventory and export-control practices (Reuters via The Star). These are company statements, not regulatory findings.
  • A broader routing pattern. Chinese customs data analysed by Nikkei show imports of US-origin chipmaking equipment traced to Singapore rising 17% to $5.7bn in 2025, and those traced to Malaysia more than doubling to $3.4bn, while direct US shipments fell by more than a third. This covers wafer-fabrication equipment, not AI servers, and shows trade routing, not violations or misleading disclosures. It illustrates how quickly high-tech trade routes reconfigure around Southeast Asian hubs as direct US shipments fall.

A second route may involve no physical transshipment at all. Chinese firms can seek computing capacity in overseas data centres, an arrangement that is not automatically prohibited but whose legality depends on the entities, equipment, services and end uses involved. The House passed the Remote Access Security Act by 369 votes to 22 in January 2026 to strengthen US authorities' ability to control such arrangements; it would need Senate approval and the President's signature to become law. Meanwhile, Oracle reportedly cancelled a $7bn deal to give Tencent access to chips in Southeast Asian data centres after US officials raised concerns, and Nvidia is reportedly requiring cloud partners to sell at least 51% of capacity to non-Chinese customers (Fortune, citing the FT).

The policy line is moving. Arrangements that may be permitted today, such as a data centre in Johor leasing capacity to China-headquartered firms, could become restricted within a single credit cycle.

03 / The four maps

Four maps, one transaction

The same AI hardware transaction can be described in four ways, and each can tell a different story.

Swipe horizontally to read the full table.

MapQuestion it answersWhat a bank typically sees
CommercialWho bought and paid for the equipment?Usually: payments, trade documents and client records
PhysicalWhere was the hardware shipped, and where is it now?Often the first destination; rarely onward movement
OwnershipWho owns or controls the equipment, the intermediary and the operator?In part, through beneficial-ownership checks
UsageWho uses the computing capacity, and for what?Seldom, unless contracts give information rights

A revenue table is one version of the commercial map. A bank may see that map well, and perhaps the first physical destination, but have little sight of onward transfers, ownership changes or the users of the computing capacity. Financial crime compliance cannot reasonably reconstruct every downstream transaction without appropriate information rights, risk-based due diligence and cooperation across functions.

Nor does the absence of public end-user data mean a bank holds no relevant evidence. Trade documents, client contracts and site data may say more than a supplier's revenue disclosure. The real challenge is to reconcile these records and judge whether the resulting controls give enough assurance. Operational risk can contribute by independently challenging control coverage and the quality of that evidence.

04 / Bank touchpoints

Where a bank sits in the chain

A bank rarely touches a chip, but it often touches the money and the paperwork around one. Four channels matter, and in each the bank sees the contract more clearly than the destination.

Swipe horizontally to read the full table.

ChannelWhat the bank seesWhat it may not see
Trade finance and payments for servers and GPUsLetters of credit, invoices, transport documents, payment routingThe end user, onward shipment after the first consignee, and whether a logistics firm is a front
Banking server integrators and distributorsCustomer due diligence, account activity, stated marketsSales through third-country resellers, and staff conduct of the kind alleged in the Supermicro case
Lending to neoclouds and data-centre operatorsFinancial statements, contracts with anchor tenants, site and power dataWho rents the capacity and where those customers are headquartered
Lending secured on chips or serversCollateral schedules and valuationsWhether the assets can be used, sold or seized if the borrower is listed or the rules change

Megaspeed shows how fast the third channel can scale. Singapore filings reported by Reuters show its assets rose from $33m in 2023 to about $3bn in 2024, on revenue of $294m and a loss of $20m (Reuters via AsiaOne). Digitimes reports that liabilities grew faster and exceeded assets by about $10m. The available reporting does not establish the sources or structure of financing behind that rapid asset growth. That is the question a credit committee would need answered.

In lending, some of that visibility can be built into the facility. Options include covenants limiting the share of capacity leased to restricted parties, periodic third-party attestations of GPU inventory and use, and audit rights over tenant lists. Borrowers will resist: neoclouds and colocation providers guard tenant identities for commercial reasons, so these terms are a negotiation, not a given. They are this note's proposals, not regulatory requirements.

The fourth channel links to an earlier Deep Dive on opaque AI financing. A Bank for International Settlements bulletin notes that neoclouds borrow against their chips. If a borrower is added to the US Entity List, or remote access is brought under export controls, that collateral may be harder to use or sell.

Ownership follows the usual lines, and so do the silos. Trade finance may see the shipping documents for a consignment of servers while a separate corporate or structured-finance team lends to the data centre that receives them. The first line owns client relationships, trade-finance checks and credit decisions. Financial crime compliance owns the sanctions and export-control framework. Operational risk's role is to aggregate exposures that no single desk sees as one, for example by linking trade-finance red flags to credit approvals for the same counterparties, and to challenge whether controls work in practice.

05 / Existing expectations

What the rules already expect of banks

US authorities have been explicit that banks are part of export-control enforcement.

  • Reporting. A joint FinCEN and BIS notice of 6 November 2023 gives US financial institutions red flags for suspected evasion of US export controls beyond Russia-related cases, and asks them to use the key term FIN-2023-GLOBALEXPORT in relevant Suspicious Activity Reports. Its list of priority technologies includes AI chips and GPUs. Its red flags include letters of credit consigned to the issuing bank rather than the end user, consignees that are mail centres, trading or logistics firms, payments tied to transshipment points, and last-minute changes in payment routing.
  • Liability. In guidance to financial institutions of 9 October 2024, BIS says its General Prohibition 10 applies to banks: they may not finance or service a transaction knowing a violation has occurred or is about to. Knowledge includes awareness of a high probability, and banks “cannot willfully self-blind or ignore such red flags.” It recommends screening against restricted-party lists and post-transaction reviews.
  • Data centres. BIS guidance on diversion of advanced chips of 13 May 2025 is aimed at industry, not banks, but its red flags read like a credit checklist: a data centre that cannot show the power and cooling needed for the servers it is buying, an infrastructure-as-a-service provider that cannot confirm its users are not headquartered in China, and extra scrutiny for sites above 10 megawatts.

US export-control exposure is not limited to a bank's US operations or dollar transactions. The 2024 guidance says General Prohibition 10 applies to financial institutions “regardless of location, country in which they are headquartered or registered, or nationality,” for items subject to the Export Administration Regulations. Items can be subject to those regulations even when made, shipped and paid for outside the United States: the guidance lists US-origin items wherever located, and certain foreign-made items that contain more than a de minimis share of controlled US content or are made with controlled US technology. EMEA banks therefore need to assess their exposure through the goods, counterparties and transactions they finance, rather than relying only on jurisdiction or payment currency.

Export controls are not the only route to liability. In the Singapore case, alleged misstatements to suppliers about who would use the servers were charged as fraud, with money-laundering charges following. Where a customer misrepresents end users in documents a bank relies on, such as a letter of credit, an invoice or a loan application, the same conduct may raise domestic fraud and money-laundering concerns, whatever the reach of US export controls.

In the EU, the EBA's guidelines on internal policies, procedures and controls for restrictive measures (EBA/GL/2024/14), issued on 14 November 2024, have applied since 30 December 2025. The management body approves the compliance strategy and oversees it (paragraph 4). Each business area's exposure to restrictive measures and vulnerability to circumvention must be assessed, covering geographic, customer, product and delivery-channel risk, including beneficial owners' links to affected jurisdictions and the vulnerabilities intermediaries create (paragraphs 22–28). EU restrictive measures and US export controls are separate obligations with different legal bases. A bank should assess applicable export controls in their own right, while sharing the customer, transaction and sanctions intelligence both depend on.

So the requirement exists. The gap is visibility. The red flags assume documents that show a consignee, a route or a payment chain. Remote access leaves no shipping trail, and a supplier's revenue disclosure may not show where its hardware sits. A bank can follow the guidance and still be unable to show what its clients' hardware is doing. That is the same gap between what a framework documents and what it can observe that runs through this series.

06 / For the committee

Five questions for a CRO or operational risk committee

  1. For our material AI hardware-related exposures, can we reconcile the contracting customer, beneficial owner, shipment destination and, where relevant and obtainable, the ultimate user of computing capacity? Where we cannot, how is the residual risk assessed and escalated?
  2. Do our trade-finance and payment controls use the FinCEN and BIS red flags for advanced computing, and what did those red flags actually catch this year?
  3. For neocloud and data-centre borrowers, do we know who rents the capacity, where those customers are headquartered and what share serves China-headquartered firms, and do our facility terms give us the information rights to find out?
  4. Where chips or servers are collateral, what happens to recovery if the borrower is added to the Entity List, remote access is restricted, or the hardware is seized?
  5. Is export-control exposure assessed in its own right, alongside our sanctions exposure assessments, and how do the first line, financial crime compliance and operational risk share the intelligence both depend on?

The risk is not simply that chips cross borders. It is that the money, the hardware, the ownership and the computing activity can cross different boundaries, while the bank's controls see only fragments of the transaction.
