Deep Dive · AI financing & operational resilience

Opaque AI financing: an operational-risk deep dive

A bank can hold a compliant supplier arrangement and still lack sight of the financing that keeps the service running. This note asks whether existing third-party rules reach that gap.

By Ben St Georges · 9 November 2026 · 8 min read

Opaque AI financing is not yet an established threat to European bank solvency. It may, however, expose a less visible vulnerability: the financial resilience of the technology infrastructure on which banks increasingly depend.

The IMF, BIS and Bank of England have documented the growing scale and complexity of AI financing, including circular investments, private credit and off-balance-sheet commitments. What remains difficult to establish is how these exposures intersect with banks' own lending, financing and third-party dependencies.

For operational risk leaders, the immediate question is practical. Could financial distress at an upstream AI infrastructure provider interrupt a critical service, even when the bank's direct supplier remains contractually obligated to deliver?

Regulatory implication: Existing ICT third-party risk requirements already address provider failure, service continuity and exit planning. What remains insufficiently understood is whether those arrangements adequately capture financial distress within the increasingly complex funding structures behind critical AI services.

01 / AI financing and credit structure

How AI is financed, and what is known

Three mechanisms make the funding hard to read. In circular deals, a vendor invests in an AI firm that then buys from the vendor. The BIS finds that close to half of AI-to-AI deal value between 2021 and 2025 was between firms that also share a commercial supply-chain relationship (BIS Bulletin 137, 1 October 2026). The BIS notes that its calculations have not been reviewed by PitchBook analysts and may differ from PitchBook's methodology, and that deal values are the full disclosed transaction value, not each investor's contribution.

In off-balance-sheet structures, a vehicle owns the data centre, issues bonds or asset-backed securities, and a hyperscaler leases the capacity. BIS Bulletin 140 (27 October 2026) cites Moody's for data-centre lease commitments of $1.2tn by mid-2026, about $820bn of them for leases that have not yet started. These are future lease commitments, not $1.2tn of outstanding financial debt. Leases that have not yet started are disclosed but not recorded as debt. Institutional investors, including private credit funds, can hold the vehicles' debt, and the BIS notes that banks often lend to both the vehicles and their investors.

The third mechanism is private credit. Private credit loans to AI firms exceeded $200bn by early 2026, almost 8% of private credit loans in US data (BIS Bulletin 120, January 2026). Because funds can both lend to AI firms and hold vehicle debt, and banks lend to the funds, the same exposure can reach a bank by more than one route.

The case that this is a solvency threat is weak so far. The BIS finds spillovers to Treasury and investment-grade markets "small and short-lived," though it warns that "sustained issuance at the current pace could change this" (Bulletin 140). The ECB describes direct bank lending to private markets as "marginal" (Financial Stability Review, May 2026), and the largest issuers look lightly leveraged on conventional measures (Barchart via Yahoo, May 2026; secondhand figures). But the BIS notes that hyperscalers' off-balance-sheet commitments "already exceed their reported debt," so those measures may understate exposure. The position looks manageable at the top on credit quality and unknown in the tail.

02 / EMEA bank exposure

Why EMEA bank exposure is hard to measure

Publicly available sources do not currently publish an AI-specific figure for EU bank exposure. The nearest is the ESAs Joint Committee's Autumn 2026 update (23 September 2026), which reports nearly €150bn of EU/EEA bank exposure to private credit funds and related asset managers, from EBA large-exposure data at June 2025 (0.6% of total assets). The ESAs call it an indicative measure that excludes exposures below the large-exposure threshold. It is neither a complete measure of private credit exposure nor an AI measure: it shows the scale of the funding channel, not how much of it is AI.

Exposure can sit in four places: direct lending and project finance, funding lines to the non-banks that hold the debt, significant risk transfers and loan sales whose buyers the published sources do not name (FT, via Resultsense), and dependency on the providers themselves. The first three are credit and treasury questions. The BIS says ultimate exposures are "concentrated in a few large firms – more than what their balance sheets suggest," and the ECB has reportedly asked a small number of lenders for detail (Bloomberg via Insurance Journal); any findings are not public. The fourth channel is where operational risk has a particularly important role. The first line owns supplier relationships and continuity controls, while operational risk provides the framework, independent challenge and aggregation of dependencies that may not be visible within individual supplier assessments.

03 / Transmission to service disruption

How financing stress could become third-party disruption

Financial distress does not automatically interrupt a service. Contracts, guarantees, restructuring finance, alternative capacity and the provider's own liquidity can each halt the chain before it reaches the service. The chain below makes those protections explicit (an illustrative mechanism, not an observed sequence or forecast).

  1. Financing shock. Borrower default, refinancing pressure or reduced investor appetite.
  2. Provider response. Restructuring, reduced investment or loss of access to financed infrastructure.
  3. Continuity controls tested. Liquidity buffers, alternative capacity, contractual protections and tested recovery arrangements. This is where strong controls can halt the chain, and where hidden funding dependencies can bypass them.
  4. Potential operational disruption. Service degradation, capacity loss, security deterioration or forced migration.

The point for a bank is that it can hold a well-documented third-party arrangement and still lack sight of the upstream financial dependencies that decide whether the service continues. When a provider restructures, the party deciding whether capacity stays funded may be a lender or vehicle owner outside the bank's contract. Much of the debt is secured on the hardware itself: BIS Bulletin 140 notes that vehicle debt is also backed by the data centre's chips and that neoclouds borrow against their chips. Collateral alone does not establish who decides whether capacity keeps running in a default. That would depend on the financing documents and the insolvency process, neither of which the bank's supplier contract governs.

Precedents are small and not AI-specific. UKCloud entered compulsory liquidation in October 2022. The Cabinet Office provided £20m of day-one funding, £2.5m of it later repaid, to underwrite the official receiver's costs, and customers were told that continuing to use the service would cost more than seven times the contract price (Civil Service World). Cyxtera entered Chapter 11 in June 2023 with a commitment for $200m of debtor-in-possession financing from certain term lenders (Financier Worldwide). In both cases, continuity during the wind-down depended on who funded it.

04 / Existing regulatory expectations

What DORA and PRA expectations already require

DORA Article 28(8) requires exit strategies for ICT services supporting critical or important functions. They must account for possible provider failure, deteriorating service quality and business disruption, and exit plans must be documented, sufficiently tested and reviewed periodically. Article 30(3)(f) requires contracts to provide for a mandatory adequate transition period during which the provider keeps delivering while the firm migrates to another provider or in-house.

DORA Article 29(2) attaches different duties to different contract features. Where a contract concerns ICT services supporting critical or important functions, firms must duly consider the insolvency law that would apply if the provider went bankrupt, and any constraint on urgently recovering their data. Where it allows the provider to subcontract those services, they must weigh the benefits and risks of doing so. Where it provides for subcontracting, they must assess whether long or complex subcontracting chains could limit their ability to monitor the contracted functions. Separately, Article 30(2)(a) requires the contract to state whether subcontracting of such services is permitted and on what conditions. The subcontracting RTS (Delegated Regulation 2025/532), made under Article 30(5), requires firms to assess the chain of subcontractors that underpin a critical service, including the impact of a possible subcontractor failure on the financial entity's digital operational resilience and financial soundness. The RTS articles reviewed do not address the entities that finance or own the underlying facilities, and whether they count as part of that chain is an open question. These expectations strengthen the case for examining upstream dependencies, although they do not necessarily reveal the financing structures underpinning those services.

Under PRA SS2/21 (the November 2024 version, in force; a March 2026 revision takes effect on 18 March 2027), paragraph 5.19 expects due diligence on material outsourcing providers to consider factors including their financial situation, and Chapter 10 expects documented exit strategies that distinguish stressed exits, following a provider's failure or insolvency, from planned exits, with stressed-exit plans tested.

Neither regime is AI-specific, and neither asks a bank to monitor a supplier's financing structure. The requirement exists; what may be missing is the visibility and assurance to show that it works for providers funded through vehicles, leases and private credit. It is the same gap between what a framework documents and what it can observe, verify and control that this series keeps returning to.

05 / For the committee

Five questions for a CRO or operational risk committee

  1. Does our third-party assurance tell us whether a provider can deliver today, or whether the underlying infrastructure can remain funded and operational under financial stress?
  2. Where our critical AI services rely on non-hyperscaler compute, neoclouds or highly leveraged SaaS vendors, does capacity sit in leased vehicles or SPVs, and who controls those assets in a default?
  3. Have our exit plans been tested against provider financial distress, not only termination for convenience, and who would fund continuity during a restructuring?
  4. What do our contracts give us on notice, data portability, audit rights and transition periods if a provider restructures, becomes insolvent or changes control?
  5. Do credit, treasury and third-party risk records share an identifier for AI infrastructure providers, and who escalates when a financing indicator changes?
