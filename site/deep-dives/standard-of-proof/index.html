Deep Dive · AI governance & operational resilience

The standard of proof

The FCA's 19 October speech and practice review add no new AI rules — but across twenty case studies they sketch a concrete picture of the evidence supervisors may expect, on consumer outcomes, governance and operational resilience alike. For institutions already assembling parallel files for the FSB, the ECB and MAS, that evidentiary signal may matter more than any rulebook would.

By Ben St Georges · 27 October 2026 · 12 min read

19 Oct 2026

The FCA publishes Jessica Rusu's speech, “Building the future of finance in the age of AI,” alongside “AI in financial services: good practice and areas for improvement.”

20 Oct 2026

Travers Smith issues a next-day briefing. From 07:00, a power failure in Google Cloud's London region disrupts services for some Revolut customers; Lloyds, Halifax and HSBC customers separately report app problems that morning, with no cause attributed.

22 Oct 2026

Ask a supervisor which single artefact would most reassure them about a firm's use of AI, and the honest answer is rarely a policy document. It is a file: a named senior manager, signed off before a model went live. A record of what was tested, on whom, and what changed as a result. A tested plan for the day a model's only supplier withdraws it.

On 19 October, the Financial Conduct Authority effectively published that file's contents page.

Jessica Rusu, the FCA's chief data, information and intelligence officer, used a speech at Bloomberg's UK Policy Series to launch AI in financial services: good practice and areas for improvement — twenty lettered case studies, drawn from supervisory engagement and the AI Lab's live-testing work, set against three themes: Consumer Duty and vulnerable customers, governance and accountability, and operational resilience. The publication is explicit that the examples are "illustrative, non-exhaustive and will evolve as practice develops," and that they "do not create new requirements, prescribe a single model or replace firms' responsibility for considering what is appropriate" to their own use of AI.

That disclaimer is true, and almost beside the point. Twenty short, concrete vignettes of what supervisors have actually seen — who got it right, who got it wrong, and why — are a more useful signal of the evidence a firm will be asked to produce than any new rule would be. "Standard of proof" is this piece's shorthand for that evidentiary signal, not a phrase the FCA uses or a formal legal test it has created. The timing gave the signal an unplanned footnote: the publication landed the day before a power failure at a single London data centre disrupted Revolut's services for some customers, putting the resilience section's warnings to a live test before the ink was dry.

01 / Context

A review built from case files, not new clauses

This is not a stand-alone initiative. It sits inside the FCA's wider programme on AI in retail financial services — the Mills Review, commissioned in January 2026 to examine how AI could reshape the sector by 2030, and the AI Lab's AI Live Testing and Supercharged Sandbox strands, which have been feeding the regulator real deployment data for over a year. The 19 October publication is the first attempt to turn that evidence into something firms can actually benchmark their own practice against.

It is also narrower than the coverage suggested. The broader cyber-security implications of frontier AI are out of scope of this iteration — a theme this publication has tracked separately through September and October. The review does, however, cover firms' own operational and cyber resilience when deploying AI. What the 19 October package covers is the ordinary conduct and governance plumbing: the controls a firm needs whether its AI is a chatbot, a credit model, or an early-stage agent.

02 / Consumer duty

The question is not whether it works — it's for whom

The Consumer Duty theme reads, at first, like familiar fair-outcomes territory: test for unjustified differences across consumer groups, keep auditable records of prompts and model versions, tell consumers when they are dealing with AI. The sharper material sits in the contrasts between case studies.

One firm tightened its guardrails after a bias review, only to find the fix made its outputs "overly generic" and steered some consumers toward inappropriate options — a correction that solved one problem by quietly creating another. Another used AI to flag spending patterns suggesting vulnerability, routed the harder calls to an ethics committee, and used the outcome to retrain the model, rather than treating the AI's first answer as final. The gap between those two firms is not technical sophistication. It is whether anyone was watching for the second-order effect.

That sentence sits in the governance theme, but it belongs here too. It undercuts the default answer most firms give a supervisor — we have a human in the loop — by naming the specific failure mode: a reviewer who rubber-stamps AI output because it looks plausible, set against one who reaches a decision before seeing what the model recommended, so there is something to actually compare it against. So-what: before a supervisor asks, test which of those two your own escalation points actually resemble.

03 / Governance

The paper trail is the control

The governance theme's anchor case studies are really one story told twice. In one, a firm deployed AI first and discovered, only once problems surfaced, that nobody agreed who owned oversight — retrofitting governance onto a live system proved far harder than building it in. In the other, a firm did the opposite: a named senior manager and a separate model owner, both documented before deployment, for every material use case. The difference the FCA is pointing at is not whether governance exists on paper somewhere. It is whether it existed, in writing, before the decision it was meant to govern.

For UK firms, "named senior manager" is not a figure of speech. The publication places this squarely inside SM&CR: its governance theme has a section on senior management functions and reasonable steps, and its good-practice examples include firms that "mapped responsibilities for AI across senior management functions." In the second case above, ultimate accountability sat with "the SMF responsible for the business area the tool would operate in," while the model owner oversaw the technical build. The FCA presents that as one firm's approach rather than a prescription — elsewhere it notes firms using tailored ownership structures — and it names no particular SMF role. Our reading is that the example still offers a useful steer on a question many firms have left open since SM&CR went live: whether AI belongs to the senior manager who runs operations and technology (often SMF24), the one who runs risk (SMF4), or the one whose customers bear the outcome. It leans toward the last — business-outcome ownership, with technical ownership beneath it — though the right answer will depend on each firm's own allocation of responsibilities. So-what: check that each material use case is traceable to the relevant senior manager's Statement of Responsibilities; where it is not, the "reasonable steps" that senior manager could point to in a review become much harder to evidence.

A third case pushes the same logic into incident response: a pre-approved playbook with red/amber/green thresholds, each tied to a predefined action — enhanced monitoring, or a temporary shutdown — agreed before anything went wrong, not improvised in the room once it had. That is the evidentiary unit the FCA appears to be building toward across all three themes: named accountability, documented pre-deployment, and a pre-agreed response — timestamped, not asserted after the fact.

It is also, not by coincidence, the unit other standard-setters have converged on this autumn, albeit with different legal force behind each one. MAS's AI Risk Management Guidelines phase in supervisory expectations — effective from October 2027, fully from October 2028 — around accountability that survives outsourcing. The FSB's sound practices, finalised on 15 October and explicitly framed as "a practical toolkit" rather than a rulebook, rest on the same organisation-wide pillars, but ask firms to adapt them, not comply with them.

04 / Agentic AI & resilience

The fallback plan is the control

The resilience theme's case studies are almost mechanical in their clarity. One firm built a product around a single model with no alternative; when the provider retired that model, the product had to be redesigned under pressure. Two others deliberately avoided single-model dependence — one kept alternate server capacity in a different location, the other designed its architecture so it could switch providers without a rebuild. The FCA ties this directly to an existing rule, not a new one: mapping AI dependencies, including externally hosted models, into resilience planning is already a requirement for firms in scope of SYSC 15A.

Agentic AI gets a shorter, more exploratory treatment — and the gaps in it matter more than what's settled. Rusu's published speech signals that the FCA wants existing major-incident reporting obligations to extend to "significant AI failures and near-misses" — a supervisory signal, not a newly enacted rule, but one that in principle brings an agent's silent misfire inside the regulatory perimeter for the first time. In practice, as HSF Kramer points out, neither the speech nor the publication defines what a near miss actually is. The promised "trusted framework" for agentic finance — covering agent identity, delegated authority, accountability and execution — has no committed timeline, and Freshfields is blunt about the hole it would need to fill: there is currently no settled, interoperable way to verify an AI agent's identity or tie it back to the person legally responsible for what it did.

That gap is not abstract. The day after the FCA's publication, a power failure in Google Cloud's London region disrupted card payments and sign-ins for some Revolut customers — exactly the kind of shared, externally hosted dependency the resilience theme asks firms to map, arriving as live evidence rather than a hypothetical case study. (Lloyds, Halifax and HSBC customers also reported app problems that morning, but the contemporaneous reporting does not link those to the same incident.) Google's preliminary incident report traces the cause to a power-grid disruption at the data centre hosting one London availability zone, where the automation meant to start the emergency generators failed and battery reserves ran out — six hours and ten minutes of disruption. Not an AI failure itself, but a reminder that the same dependency sits underneath AI workloads too.

05 / The EMEA read-across

One evidentiary standard, several clocks

Line up this month's instruments and a pattern appears that none of them states outright. The FCA wants a named accountable owner, pre-deployment sign-off and a tested fallback — evidenced through Consumer Duty and conduct, via illustrative examples that create no new requirement. The FSB's sound practices want the same governance and lifecycle controls, framed explicitly as a non-binding toolkit firms are "encouraged" to adapt. The ECB's instrument carries more legal weight: a letter from the Supervisory Board chair to the CEOs of the significant institutions it supervises directly, invoking DORA's existing requirements and asking for a cyber action plan addressing AI-enabled threats, due to the firm's Joint Supervisory Team by Saturday 31 October — the end of this week. MAS sets the most explicit phased supervisory expectations, running from October 2027 through October 2028. Four instruments, different legal force and clocks — and, underneath, substantially one question: can this firm name who is accountable for this AI, show what was tested, and prove it has a plan for when the thing it depends on fails.

The risk for an EMEA group operating under several of these regimes at once is building four compliance work-streams to answer one question four times. The more useful read is the opposite: one shared evidence pack — built once to the toughest common standard any of these bodies has set, then mapped to each regime's own scope, legal force and deadline — is likely to answer most of them simultaneously. The work is less about satisfying the FCA, the ECB and MAS in turn than about deciding, once, what that pack needs to contain — and then being able to produce it, mapped to whichever regime is asking, in an afternoon rather than reconstructed under supervisory pressure.

A minimum viable evidence pack

This piece's synthesis of the case studies and commentary above — not a list any regulator has published.

  • 01 Accountability record. An accountable SMF — in the FCA's example, the one responsible for the business area the use case serves — traceable to their Statement of Responsibilities, plus a separate technical model owner — both documented and dated before go-live.
  • 02 Outcome testing that outlasts the fix. Results across consumer groups, including vulnerable customers, before deployment and again after any guardrail change — with auditable records of prompts and model versions — so a bias fix that quietly degrades advice quality shows up.
  • 03 Evidence of independent human review. Proof that high-risk cases are decided before reviewers see the model's recommendation, with a sample of where reviewer and model disagreed.
  • 04 Incident playbook. Red/amber/green thresholds, each tied to a predefined action up to temporary shutdown — and a working definition of "near miss" the firm has set for itself, since the FCA has not.
  • 05 Dependency map and substitution plan. Every external model and hosting location mapped into SYSC 15A important business services (and, for EU entities, the DORA register of ICT third-party arrangements), with a tested route to switch provider or fail over.

Worked scenario — a cumulative maturity ladder

  • L1 Retrofitted. Deployed before governance was agreed; ownership was still being argued when problems surfaced.
  • L2 Signed off, then left. A named SMF and separate model owner documented before go-live — but oversight largely stops at approval, the pattern Deloitte describes.
  • L3 Governed across the lifecycle. Pre-deployment sign-off, plus live thresholds with predefined actions, independent human review and a tested substitution plan — the full pack above, retrievable on request.

Which level does your most material AI use case reach today — and could you prove it?

For the board

  1. Pre-deployment sign-off. For every AI use case rated material, can you produce the senior manager's sign-off dated before deployment — not reconstructed after the fact?
  2. Substantive challenge. Could your second- and third-line reviewers describe the model's known failure modes, or would they only confirm that its outputs "looked reasonable"?
  3. Near-miss capture. If an AI agent generated a near miss tomorrow, would your incident process capture it — given the FCA itself has not yet defined what a near miss is?
  4. Single-model dependency. Does any current, material AI use case depend on a single model or provider with no tested substitution plan?
  5. Day-one retrievability. Is your evidence of governance — sign-offs, thresholds, escalation logs — retrievable within a day, or does it exist mainly as a policy statement?
  6. Cross-regime mapping. Where the same use case sits inside FCA, ECB and MAS scope at once, does one evidence pack answer all three — mapped to each regime's own legal force and deadline — or are three programmes quietly building three different answers to the same question?

Close

The file, not the framework

The FCA has not asked firms to build a new control. It has shown, through twenty short files, a concrete picture of the evidence it wants to see that the old ones work — and, read alongside the FSB, the ECB and MAS, it has made the underlying question harder to avoid: not whether a firm's AI governance exists, but whether it can be produced, on request, before anyone needs to ask twice.
